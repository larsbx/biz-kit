# Trivium and Quadrivium teaching model

This is the organizing framework selected by the user. Our first application is
[second-grade mathematics](../curriculum/mathematics/grade-2/README.md).

## Trivium: language, reasoning, and expression

| Art | Work in this course | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grammar | Learn the names, symbols, definitions, and relationships of the topic; connect words to objects and diagrams | Correct naming, reading, representation, and recalled relationships |
| Logic or dialectic | Compare, classify, ask why, test a claim, and identify an error or counterexample | A reason or model that justifies the result |
| Rhetoric | Present an explanation that another person can follow, using speech, drawings, demonstrations, and later writing | Clear communication linking the claim, representation, and reason |

Use all three in each lesson at a suitable level. For a young learner, reasoning
may be a concrete exchange of counters and rhetoric a short spoken demonstration.
Grammar work also includes practice and recall. The table describes how we adapt
these disciplines to mathematical learning; the full language arts are broader.

## Quadrivium: four connected mathematical arts

| Art | Mathematical study | First-course application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arithmetic | Number, amount, operations, and numerical relationships | Counting, place value, composition/decomposition, parity, and calculations |
| Geometry | Form, magnitude, position, and measurement | Draw/build shapes; compare lengths; arrange equal squares; construct equal shares |
| Music | A historical member of the Quadrivium | Excluded from this curriculum by user direction |
| Astronomy | The ordered changes and motions observed in the sky | Keep dated moon/day-night observations, compare intervals, and reason about repeated cycles |

Arithmetic is the first course's main strand. Geometry and astronomy have
planned investigations and later units. A lesson may focus on one art; connections
should arise from actual mathematical work. Calendars and weeks are human conventions
for keeping time and must be distinguished from observed astronomical cycles.

## Example: explaining an unchanged amount

Start with 23 counters. Exchange ten ones for a bundle of ten while keeping the
total fixed. Name the units and represent 23; explain why regrouping changes the
representation without changing the amount; teach that explanation to another
person. Extend the idea by arranging counters in equal rows and explaining why
the arrangement preserves the count.

This develops the concept behind place value, a reason for the exchange, and a
communicable explanation. The lesson task and assessment each make those practices
visible.

## Progression with modern usage

Begin with the counting unit and relationships among numbers. Develop composition,
difference, equal grouping, sharing, and comparison; apply those relationships to
geometric magnitude and form, then to recorded astronomical changes and intervals.
Each inquiry uses grammar, reasoning, and explanation. Review prerequisites as
relationships are extended, rather than treating an age label as proof of readiness.

Modern notation, objects, measuring tools, diagrams, everyday examples, and online
practice are useful in this progression. Choose them for their contribution to
understanding. The user welcomed modern usage and excluded music; see
[decision 0004](decisions/0004-modern-usage-classical-progression.md) and
[decision 0005](decisions/0005-no-music.md).

## Planning and review

For each lesson, record the primary art, names/notation, reasoning question,
explanation task, practice, and evidence. Use entry work to choose the number range
and amount of review. Apply the [readiness requirements for the record type](curriculum-model.md#readiness-by-record-type):
teaching and assessment records need usable tasks and evidence criteria; resources
need source, access, and suitability review. Keep actual learner evidence privately.

## Historical vocabulary and our adaptation

Isidore's *Etymologies* I.2 lists the seven liberal arts, and Book III discusses
arithmetic, music, geometry, and astronomy. These primary texts anchor the names;
the second-grade activities and teaching model above are our adaptation, rather
than a historical author's prescribed curriculum for this age.

- [Isidore, Etymologies I.2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Isidore/1%2A.html#2)
- [Isidore, Etymologies III](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Isidore/3%2A.html)

Historical reference consulted on 2026-10-09. The names retain their historical
meaning; our selected curriculum omits music. Empirical astronomy tasks will use
reviewed modern sources and actual observations when authored.
