## Prerequisites

For the two-digit route, explain tens/ones and a zero ones position using lesson
1's models. For the three-digit route, also require lesson 2's explanation of
hundreds. Choose the corresponding activities below.

## Grammar

Distinguish the amount, its spoken name, its numeral, and a sum of place values.
Introduce expanded form as another representation of the same amount.

## Logic

Test the claim that 608 = 60 + 8 by building both amounts. Find the error in
which unit the 6 represents.

## Rhetoric

Present one amount in three forms and explain why all three refer to the same quantity.

## Activities

1. Retrieval: model 305 and explain its zero position (3 minutes).
2. Model: connect 257, two hundred fifty-seven, and 200 + 50 + 7 (5 minutes).
3. Supported work: convert 340 and 608 between forms (7 minutes).
4. Independent work: write five hundred three as a numeral and expanded form (7 minutes).
5. Exit: present 460 in words and expanded form, then explain the 6 (3 minutes).

## Teacher key and next step

- 340: three hundred forty; 300 + 40.
- 608: six hundred eight; 600 + 8 (a zero-tens term is optional).
- Five hundred three: 503; 500 + 3.
- 460: four hundred sixty; 400 + 60; the 6 contributes six tens.
- Accept an accurate spoken explanation even when spelling needs support.

## Two-digit route

Use this route when only ones/tens have been taught. It replaces the three-digit
examples above; hundreds and the thousand position remain pending.

| Stage | Task and mathematical work | Minutes | Teacher key |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieval | Model 40 and explain its zero position | 3 | 4 tens, 0 ones |
| Model | Connect 57, its spoken name, and its place-value sum | 5 | Fifty-seven; 50 + 7 |
| Supported work | Convert 60 and 68 between forms; test the claim that the 6 in 68 means six single ones | 7 | Sixty = 60; sixty-eight = 60 + 8; the 6 records six tens |
| Independent work | Write thirty-nine as a numeral and place-value sum | 7 | 39; 30 + 9 |
| Exit | Name 82, write its expanded form, and explain the 8 | 3 | Eighty-two; 80 + 2; eight tens |

Seek agreement among the numeral, spoken name, sum, and model. Accept accurate
spoken names without requiring independent spelling. Return to concrete tens/ones
if a digit is treated as single ones regardless of its place.

## Optional thousand extension

Teach this separate activity only after lesson 2's hundred exchange and the
three-digit forms above have been demonstrated. Before starting, ask the learner
to build 200, name the units, and explain why one hundred can be unpacked into
ten tens. If the unit relationships are uncertain, return to lesson 2. This
activity extends outcomes 001 and 003; it is outside the two-digit core route.
Allow another 25 minutes or split the work; these are authoring estimates.

Use ten hundred flats or ten established groups, each containing ten tens made
from ten ones each. Keep their unit value visible. Use a container or band to
enclose those ten groups as one thousand-group, or exchange them for a thousand
block with the same value. A token alone is insufficient until its represented
amount has been established. Use a chart headed Thousands, Hundreds, Tens, Ones.

### Worked examples and concrete exchange

1. **Name and count the groups (3 minutes).** Place nine hundred-groups on the
   table. Count 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900. Add one more
   hundred-group: ten groups of 100 ones make 1,000 ones. Name the difference
   between the number of groups (ten) and the number of ones (one thousand).
2. **Model the exchange and the fourth place (5 minutes).** Enclose the ten
   hundred-groups in one thousand-group. Open it again to check its contents.
   Explain: ten hundreds and one thousand both contain 1,000 of the same ones;
   packaging changes the grouped unit, while the amount remains unchanged.
   Record the exchange as 10 hundreds = 1 thousand = 1,000 ones. On the chart,
   place 1 in Thousands and 0 in each lower column. Write 1,000; read "one
   thousand"; give its place-value sum as 1,000 + 0 + 0 + 0 (or simply 1,000).
   Compare 900's three positions with 1,000's four: the 1 occupies the new place
   to the left of Hundreds. The comma separates digits and is not a digit position.
   The three zeros record no additional hundreds, tens, or ones outside the
   thousand-group; they do not mean that the amount contains no ones.
3. **Supported grouping and explanation (7 minutes).** Give one set of four
   hundred-groups and another of six. Ask the learner to combine them, exchange
   the ten hundreds for one thousand, then unpack it again. Ask: "Did putting
   them into one group change the amount? How can we check?" Test the claim
   "Nine hundreds can also be exchanged for one thousand without changing the
   amount" using nine groups and the required ten. Finish with a teach-back:
   point to the objects, each chart column, the numeral, name, and sum and explain
   their agreement to another person.

### Independent practice and exit check

Allow 7 minutes for the following practice, without answer cues. Objects,
drawings, and spoken explanations are allowed.

| Practice | Learner task |
| --- | --- |
| T3-I1 | Unpack one thousand-group into hundreds. Then unpack every hundred into tens. Record the number of groups at each stage and explain why the amount stays the same. |
| T3-I2 | Hear "one thousand." Fill a blank Thousands/Hundreds/Tens/Ones chart, write its numeral and a sum of place values, and explain each zero. |
| T3-I3 | A person says, "After ten hundreds are packed into one group, there are only 100 ones." Use the contents of the group to test the claim and explain the error. |

Exit check (3 minutes): show six hundred-groups beside four hundred-groups.
Ask whether together they can fill one thousand-group exactly. Require a model
and a preservation reason; then ask which place records the new grouped unit.

### Extension teacher key and next step

| Stage or item | Teacher key or model evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Prerequisite | 200 = 2 hundreds; each hundred can be unpacked into 10 tens, each ten into 10 ones |
| Worked exchange | 9 hundreds = 900; adding 1 hundred gives 10 hundreds = 1,000 ones = 1 thousand. Enclosing or exchanging equal-valued groups adds/removes no ones |
| Supported task | 4 hundreds + 6 hundreds = 10 hundreds = 1 thousand. Nine hundreds contain 900 ones, 100 fewer than a thousand, so that exchange would change the amount |
| T3-I1 | 1 thousand = 10 hundreds = 100 tens = 1,000 ones. The number of groups changes with the chosen unit; the amount does not |
| T3-I2 | Chart: 1 thousand, 0 hundreds, 0 tens, 0 ones; numeral 1,000; sum 1,000 or 1,000 + 0 + 0 + 0. Four digit positions; zeros mean no additional lower-place groups |
| T3-I3 | False: each of the 10 hundreds contains 100 ones, so the packed group contains 1,000 ones, not 100. The outer packaging does not remove its contents |
| Exit | 6 hundreds + 4 hundreds = 1 thousand exactly; equal-valued packaging preserves 1,000 ones. Thousands is the new position left of Hundreds |

Seek correct unit names, equal amounts before and after exchange, a matching
four-position representation, and an explanation another person can follow.
If any relationship needs review, reopen the concrete group and reconnect the
objects and chart before proceeding. Then teach lesson 4's
[separate count through 1,000](04-counting.md#optional-count-through-1000).
PVX becomes a taught-scope supplement only after both activities and their checks;
no learner performance is inferred from their inclusion here.
